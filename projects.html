Ebike Project

Ongoing, self directed build

Electro Mechanical Design Python Field Oriented Control Motor Simulation Data Visualization Matplotlib Pandas / NumPy Regenerative Braking CAD

Background

This project started with my dad. He wanted to ride around our neighborhood in Georgetown, Texas, but the hills there make for a rough ride. An ebike felt like the fix, so I decided to design and build the powertrain myself.

Choosing the Hardware

  • Followed my mentor Bhargav's build outline and bought the same budget bike he used.
  • Weighed a mid drive motor (true pedal torque sensing) against a rear hub motor (simpler, but needs a steel torque arm and regenerative braking to stay competitive).
  • Planned ahead for upgraded brakes and a battery mount to handle the added weight.

Bench Testing Both Motors

  • Ran both motors through a simulator, sweeping every gear for the mid drive and every throttle level for the hub motor.
  • Found the same issue everywhere: efficiency collapsed and simulated temperatures spiked at low speed, since a stalled DC motor turns unused current into heat.
  • Traced this to how EVs like Tesla avoid it: gating current at low speed instead of letting it all through at once.

Modeling Real World Performance

  • No dataset online paired torque with motor power, so I calculated torque myself from speed and grade at every timestamp.
  • My first comparison had the mid drive winning everything, until I realized the model ignored the hub motor's regenerative braking. Adding 60% regen on downhills changed the picture.

Solving the Heat Problem

  • Confirmed the heat spike was real, caused by Power Loss = I²R: without wheel speed to generate back EMF, current and heat skyrocket when the motor stalls.
  • Wrote a Virtual Speed Dependent Transformer algorithm in software: choke current at low RPM, then open to full power as back EMF takes over.

Results & Takeaways

The virtual transformer logic cut heat loss at a dead stop from 80.0W to 1.8W (97.8% reduction), with zero performance penalty once moving. Once regen was factored in, the hub motor and mid drive landed at nearly identical overall efficiency (82.0% vs 82.1%), with the hub motor actually using less energy per mile in the real world model, 14.2 Wh vs 15.8 Wh, thanks to regen recapturing energy on hills.

RC Car Project

[Add timeframe here]

Controls Mechatronics CAD / SolidWorks Prototyping

Background

I've always wanted to get into cars, but breaking into automotive engineering as a hobby isn't cheap, since real parts, real tools, and real mistakes all cost real money. An RC car gave me a way to explore that world at a fraction of the scale and the cost.

Once I decided on an RC car, the next question was what kind of drivetrain to build around. With a limited budget, going with a simple electric powertrain, essentially a scaled down EV system, was the most practical choice. It let me focus on learning the fundamentals of motors, controllers, and drivetrain design without the complexity (or expense) of an internal combustion setup.

Design & Build

  • I started with a base RC chassis kit and built out the rest of the car from there, then started CADing individual components I could swap in and out to test how each one affects performance.
  • As an example, I modeled a custom wheel in CAD and mounted it onto the RC car in place of the stock wheel, aiming to see how the design change would affect handling and speed compared to the original part.
  • I'm currently in the process of testing how this wheel changes performance relative to the stock wheels it replaced.
  • I also plan on testing the wheel across different materials to see how each one performs, looking at factors like weight and durability to find the best setup.
